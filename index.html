Teacher support arrives at the wrong time

It comes as a workshop in the holidays, a policy document, or a subject advisor once a year.

Almost nothing reaches a teacher while they are planning Tuesday's lesson, and almost nobody gives them feedback on their own work.

Teachers are not short of information. They are short of a colleague who has time.

Three arms

Content. Subject knowledge and the debates behind it, built on the material already in Kula Revise.

Practice. Lessons a teacher can actually teach, with a short conversation before and after.

Assessment. Marking and moderating, built on what examiners credit.

This prototype is the practice arm. It is what gets a teacher to open the app at all, because it gives them something they need tomorrow morning.

We start with Grade 12 History, where our material and our examining experience already are, and expand subject by subject. Practice and reflection work for any teacher from day one; only the content and the marking judgement are built per subject.

Where the AI sits

Before Reads your check-in and suggests one thing to work on, and where in today's lesson it will matter.

During Nothing. No AI runs while you teach. The lesson and every alternative are already on the phone, so there is no data cost and nothing to wait for.

After Takes you back through what you kept, your two stars and a wish, and where the lesson ran long. It asks the questions; you do the thinking. It never grades the lesson.

Across lessons Holds you to the one thing you said you would try, and sees what one lesson cannot: the same problem in the same place, a wish you keep repeating. It keeps a short record per lesson, not a transcript.

Yours to see Read it, change it, delete it. It stays on your phone. No school or district sees a named teacher's record: the moment reflection becomes evidence, honest answers stop.

It also drafts the lessons, from Kula Revise material, with a subject specialist checking each one.

How we are building the mentor

Ask, then check. It says what it noticed and asks whether that is right. If the teacher says no, it drops its reading and works from theirs instead.

Only what the teacher gave it. What they kept, their two stars and a wish, where the lesson ran long. Never a verdict on the lesson as a whole.

One question past the first answer. The first explanation is usually the easy one. It asks once more, then stops.

End with one thing to try. Small enough to do in the next lesson, and counted the next time round.

What this lesson is really about

Three leaders signed the same words and each meant something different by them. That is not dishonesty. It is how agreements get made, and it is why the alliance broke.

They should leave able to say

  • What each leader wanted at Yalta, and why: Stalin a safe border, Roosevelt help against Japan and the UN, Churchill a free Poland.
  • Why Stalin wanted friendly neighbours, using the numbers: 27 million Soviet dead, and the war fought on Soviet land.
  • That "free elections" meant one thing to Stalin and another to Roosevelt, and both signed it.

What they usually get wrong

Learners want someone to blame, so they decide Stalin was lying. Move them past this. The words of the agreement were unclear on purpose. Clear words would have stopped all three from signing it.

What earns marks

A fact with a because. "The alliance was breaking because they disagreed" earns almost nothing. "Because 27 million Soviet citizens had died and Stalin would not accept unfriendly neighbours" earns the mark.

You need

A board, and chalk or a marker. Learners need exercise books and pens. Nothing to print, nothing to project.
