Teacher support arrives at the wrong time

It comes as a workshop in the holidays, a policy document, or a subject advisor once a year.

Almost nothing reaches a teacher while they are planning Tuesday's lesson, and almost nobody looks at the work they actually do.

Support is not missing. It arrives too far from the classroom to change what happens in it.

What happens when subject knowledge is thin

Results follow it. The first nationally representative South African study to test teachers' own subject knowledge found a positive effect on learner performance in both poorer and wealthier schools. Its own conclusion matters as much: knowing the subject is necessary, but being able to transfer it meaningfully matters more.Shepherd, 2015

And it is common. South African secondary teachers are regularly assigned subjects they were never trained to teach, and the social sciences are a common destination for it.De Wet, 2004; Du Plessis & Mestry, 2019

In the room it looks like this. A teacher who is unsure reads from the textbook, talks for longer, and sets copying. Unexpected questions get closed down. Marking becomes ticks rather than corrections. The lesson turns into memorisation, not because anyone chose that, but because it is the safe option.

Content and pedagogy are not two products. They are the two things that go wrong together.

Two arms

Content. Full, detailed lessons that cover the curriculum, with the teaching strategies to use in the classroom, written per subject. Built on the material already in Kula Revise. We start with History and expand into other subjects.

Pedagogy. Lessons a teacher can actually teach, with a short conversation before and after.

This prototype is the pedagogy arm. It is what gets a teacher to open the app at all, because it gives them something they need tomorrow morning.

We start with Grade 12 History, where our material and our examining experience already are, and expand subject by subject. Pedagogy and reflection work for any teacher from day one; only the content is built per subject.

Marking and moderating is the natural third arm, and it is where our examining experience compounds. We are not building it inside this programme.

Where the AI sits

Before Reads your check-in and suggests one thing to work on, and where in today's lesson it will matter.

During Nothing. No AI runs while you teach. The lesson and every alternative are already on the phone, so there is no data cost and nothing to wait for.

After Takes you back through what you kept, your two stars and a wish, and where the lesson ran long. It asks the questions; you do the thinking. It never grades the lesson.

Across lessons Holds you to the one thing you said you would try, and sees what one lesson cannot: the same problem in the same place, a wish you keep repeating. It keeps a short record per lesson, not a transcript.

Yours to see Read it, change it, delete it. It stays on your phone. No school or district sees a named teacher's record: the moment reflection becomes evidence, honest answers stop.

The Alliance: Yalta, 1945

Ready to teach?

Starter 7 min A group project everyone remembered differently.

Main 30 min Yalta, what the war cost, the two meanings of free elections, then a PEEL paragraph.

Plenary 3 min One written question, answered in a sentence.

You need: a board, and chalk or a marker. Learners need exercise books and pens. Nothing to print, nothing to project.

In class you will see one step at a time. You can go back a step at any point.

Before you teach

Read this before you go in

What this lesson is really about

Three leaders signed the same words and each meant something different by them. That is not dishonesty. It is how agreements get made, and it is why the alliance broke.

They should leave able to say

  • What each leader wanted at Yalta, and why: Stalin a safe border, Roosevelt help against Japan and the UN, Churchill a free Poland.
  • Why Stalin wanted friendly neighbours, using the numbers: 27 million Soviet dead, and the war fought on Soviet land.
  • That "free elections" meant one thing to Stalin and another to Roosevelt, and both signed it.

What they usually get wrong

Learners want someone to blame, so they decide Stalin was lying. Move them past this. The words of the agreement were unclear on purpose. Clear words would have stopped all three from signing it.

What earns marks

Evidence that is explained. They write a PEEL paragraph at the end of the lesson, and the sentence most of them leave out is the one saying why the evidence proves the point. "They disagreed" is not evidence. "27 million Soviet citizens had died, so Stalin would not accept unfriendly neighbours" is.

Revising the content takes about four minutes. Skip it if you know this well.
